Chapter 01 · Growth

Answer.AI

From a shared answer machine to a personalized school — and from a tool students used alone to a place where someone else is there.

Bella (Yuanyuan) Li · Product Designer · Aug 2024 – present

Okay, Answer.AI. It's an AI learning app with about seven million students. I joined in 2024 as the product designer, and this is the story of how we found growth again after hitting a wall.
The product

Scan any problem,
learn from the answer.

Photograph a homework problem, get a step-by-step solution.

7 million students. I led the full redesign, direction to ship.

To start with the product itself: the core loop is really simple. You scan a problem, and you get a step-by-step answer. That's what students came to us for, and for a while, that was genuinely enough.

Our users are seven million Gen Z students — about five million in high school and two million in college. Most of them are in mid-American towns, where instant help was either expensive or far away. And what they're facing is always the same: tonight's homework, Friday's test, and the GPA that decides where they get in. So their typical flow is simple: I'm stuck on this one, I snap a photo, I get the answer — and if I still don't get it, I ask a follow-up.
The honeymoon · 2023 — 2024

We landed in a golden window.

Free chatbots couldn't take a photo yet — GPT-4o only opened image and file upload to free users in May 2024, with caps. Snap a problem, get the steps: we owned that moment, and so did Question AI and Gauth — all of us in the App Store top 15.

Curve illustrative · milestones are real
So here's how it started. You might be wondering — how does a photo-solving app get that many users? Well, it's because in 2023 we landed in a golden window. Back then, the free chatbots couldn't read a photo yet — GPT-4o only opened image upload to free users in May 2024. So if you could snap a problem and get the steps, you owned that moment. We did, and so did Question AI and Gauth, and all of us were sitting in the App Store top fifteen. By September we'd hit six million users.
The turn · late 2024

The frontier models caught up.

Snap a photo, get the answer — free, in any chatbot. What we charged for had become table stakes. Total user growth slowed.

And then, look at the tail — the curve bent. What happened is that free ChatGPT could read photos now, competitors like Gauth started giving away free credits, and on our side, our loop ended at the answer. So we had acquisition, but no habit, and retention slipped with it.
Success metrics

Revenue was falling. Which part of it?

We were still filling the bucket. It was leaking faster than we filled it. Acquisition buys you this month. Retention buys you every month after.

Revenue for a subscription app breaks into four things: new users, how many convert, how long they stay, and price — minus what it costs to serve them, which for an AI product means every answer burns inference. [space]
So which one moved? Revenue was falling. New users were still coming — we kept spending on acquisition. Conversion was holding. Price was fixed, and not a design lever anyway. The one that was slipping was retention. [space]
And look at the order of it: retention slipped first — twenty-six percent down to eighteen — and revenue followed a month or two behind, from about thirteen thousand dollars a day down to nine. Meanwhile, on the right: new users were still edging up — acquisition was doing its job. But paying subscribers dropped sharply over the same months. We were pouring people in and losing them faster than we filled. [space]
In other words, we were still filling the bucket and it was leaking faster than we filled it. And retention is the one worth fixing first, because acquisition only buys you this month — retention buys you every month after.

Raise retention — that's what holds revenue.

So: where do we differentiate from ChatGPT?

So the goal was retention — raise it, and revenue holds. Concretely: do students open it today, tomorrow, next month, and keep paying for it? [space]
And here's the uncomfortable part. Snap a photo, get the answer — the big models do that for free now. So retention can't come from a slightly better answer; it has to come from something the answer alone doesn't give. Which turned the goal into a search: where do we differentiate from ChatGPT?

Three credible paths. None of them obviously the one.

So to answer that, we went back to the students — thirteen interviews, looking for what a chatbot wasn't giving them. [space] And out of those, we could see three directions. The first was SAT and ACT prep — the big tests. The second was college admissions — a lot of our students are first-generation college applicants, the school counselor is basically never available, and their parents can't really help. And the third was daily learning — the everyday need every student still has. [hover a card] And each of these had real commercial proof behind it — Acely, Crimson, Quizlet. So all three were real opportunities, and now it was on me and the product team to decide the direction and the priority.

I looked at it through two lenses. The first is strategic fit. I set up four questions and ran all three directions through them: does it fix our growth problem, does it reach the users we already have, does it build on our right to win, and does it keep all students in the picture. That last one is something I brought up myself — exam prep and admissions would read as "not for me" to two million college users, and I didn't want to lose them. And when you lay it all out, daily learning is the only one that clears every bar. [space → tab 2]
But I didn't want to decide on a matrix alone, so the second lens is behavior. I proposed two lightweight probes, to twenty percent of users, over two weeks. [hover] The AP prep demo got some real interest, but only four percent of students finished it. The Counselor and Scholar tutors got decent taps, but almost nobody came back. And for daily learning, I could already see it in the data: ninety-eight percent of students were using Scan to Learn, and fifty-six percent were asking a follow-up. So the way I read it: the behavior was already there. What was missing was the loop.
The direction

Daily learning became our first priority.

It was the most frequent need, closest to existing user behavior, and the strongest foundation for future growth.

The goal was to move from solving one question to a personalized school that supports how students learn every day.

That's how daily learning became our first priority. It was the most frequent need, it was closest to what students were already doing, and it was the strongest foundation for everything else we might build later. [space] So we wrote the goal down in one sentence: move from solving one question to supporting how students learn every day. And everything I'm about to show you serves that sentence.

To make daily learning actually work, I proposed this strategy to the team — basically going from a shared answer machine to a school that knows each student. There are four layers here, and each one stands on the one below it. [hover the rows] The core is about making the answer worth learning from. Stay is about giving students reasons to come back. Reach is about telling people we're not just an answer tool anymore. And Personal is gradually building a school that remembers each student. Layer one is the core — photo-solving, where every student starts. This is what it looked like: one long block of text, easy to copy, hard to learn from, and a toolbar of features sitting inside the chat. You can click the screen to see it up close. [space]
Before redesigning it, we had to agree on what a good answer even is. So we talked to teachers and looked at the homework students brought us. To figure that out we sat in on a maths class at a school here in New York — the teacher has been doing this for twenty-three years — and talked with her and a few of her students afterwards. Four things came back, and I'll let them say it. The first changed the whole layout: a teacher's board work is not a wall of text — in class they write while they talk, so each line starts with the move and the reason, and the working comes after. And the one I'd underline: a well-written answer is itself the teaching — the steps are the lesson, and on take-home sheets the teacher writes those steps out so students can follow them. Third, students want to ask right at the step where they get stuck. And fourth, teachers don't stop at the explanation — they hand over a similar question, or send a video when the explanation isn't landing. [space]
So that's what we built. The approach leads, the answer sits right below it, each step opens on tap, and every answer now ends with a next move — ask, practise, or watch. [hover the dots · click to zoom] [space]
So what moved. Before the redesign the only thing you could do after an answer was ask a follow-up, and twenty-three percent of answers got one. Now an answer ends with a next move — follow-up, a similar question, or a video — and sixty-two percent of answers lead to one of them. And one in four students now keeps a mistake book. Both of those behaviours track retention: students who do either come back at about thirty-one percent at day thirty, against fourteen percent for students who don't. I'd flag that's a correlation — those students are more invested to begin with — which is why we also ran the A/B against the old answer. Retention came up, students got their answer just as fast, and we didn't burn extra credits per answer. So: better learning, same speed, same cost.
Layer 02 · Stay · the reason they renew

Give students a reason to come back tomorrow.

Two of these were picked for proven demand: flashcards and mock practice are what students already do — in our interviews, and in the numbers of the tools they use, like Quizlet. The third, online study rooms, was a bet on a rising habit: studying with company.

Why study rooms · A trend, not a guess

“Learn with me” was already how students studied.

Our EdTech marketing agent flagged it: study-with-me streams were trending on TikTok and Instagram — students working alongside strangers for company and accountability. We built that feeling into the app.

Layer two is about staying. Three things: flashcards for daily memory, mock tests for exams, and study rooms for company while you work. [hover a card] Each of them is performing well, but the last one is the one I want to spend time on. Flashcards, for example — students who build a deck in their first week retain eleven percent better. Mock tests: sessions go up thirty-eight percent in exam weeks, and those users convert to Pro at twice the rate. And study rooms — students who join one spend twenty-eight percent longer in the app, and our most focused student sat in a room for five and a half hours straight. [click Study rooms]
You might be wondering — why would we build this feature? It started as a signal from our marketing AI agent: "study with me" was already a huge trend. It's over forty billion views on TikTok, and Lofi Girl's study stream has something like forty thousand people in it at any given moment. And it's commercial too — there's a new study-with-friends app called Focustown, and it's already making around thirty K a month. So we built that feeling into the app, and students have really taken to it — average time in the app went up noticeably for students who join rooms, and our most active student sat in a room and focused for five and a half hours straight.

Layer two is about staying, and it took us two goes. We put those features where study features usually go, in their own tab. And entry to that hub stayed flat. The features were fine; nobody was switching modes to go and study. [space]
And the reason is a mismatch between what we built and why they come. They come to finish homework — photo in, answer out. Their peak is eight to eleven at night, a problem away from done. A tab waits to be chosen, and studying was never why they opened the app. [space]
So version two: the same features, moved into the flow they already use, and offered by what just happened. Concept worth keeping — flashcards. Exam coming up, or they just covered this material — a mock. They say they're staying to work — a room. [space]
And the move worked. When the features lived in their own tab, six percent of weekly actives touched one; offered in the flow, it's twenty-one. And thirty-one percent of answers now end with the student taking one of those offers. We ran it as an A/B, so this isn't just the keen students self-selecting: the group that got in-flow offers held twenty-two percent at thirty days against eighteen — four points, on exactly the metric this whole project was about.

A light reward system keeps it going: scanning problems, mastering topics and finishing Ace’s exercises earn badges — plus random outfit drops for an avatar. The avatar matters because of where it shows up: the study room, where other students can see it. Rewards you wear in front of someone work differently from rewards you collect alone.

And layer four is personal. To make anything personal, we first had to actually know the student — so step one is collecting their data. Before, we asked students to fill in a Me page themselves, and almost nobody did. Now the tutors pick it up naturally from conversation: what they're studying, what they're struggling with, what they're aiming for. [hover step 1 for the numbers · space]
And once we know them, step two is acting on it — a check-in after a hard question, a preview the night before class, or a call with Alma when it's time to plan for college. [hover step 2] Personal messages get tapped about four times more than generic reminders. [space → tab 2]
And on top of that, we added a small incentive system — badges and an avatar — just to help students feel like this is their own personal school, not a tool they use.
A takeaway

Interviews are polite. Behaviour is honest.

We tested AP prep and a set of specialist agents. Every interview was glowing — useful, they'd use it weekly. Behaviour said otherwise, and those bets moved down the roadmap.

Interviews answer why. Data answers whether.

Ask in an interview
  • Context — what their day actually looks like
  • Workarounds — what they built to get around us
  • Language — the words they use for it
  • Emotion — what they're proud of, what embarrasses them
  • The why behind a number — why the funnel drops
Read in the data
  • Demand — a fake door beats ten interviews
  • Scale — how many, how often
  • Winner — which version actually wins
  • Willingness — what they pay for
  • Impact — whether the change moved anything

Ask what they did last week, not what they'd do next month.

One takeaway from this project. We tested AP prep and a set of specialist agents, and every interview was glowing — useful, they'd use it weekly. Behaviour said otherwise, and those bets moved down the roadmap. [space]
What I took from it isn't "don't do research" — it's that the two answer different questions. Interviews answer why. Data answers whether. So I use interviews for context, for the workarounds people invent, for the words they use, for where the emotion is. And when the question is "should we build this at all", I'd rather spend a day on a fake door than a week on interviews. Ask what they did last week, not what they'd do next month.
