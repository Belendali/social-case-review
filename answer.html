Chapter 01 · Growth

Answer.AI

From a shared answer machine to a personalized school — and from a tool students used alone to a place where someone else is there.

Bella (Yuanyuan) Li · Product Designer · Aug 2024 – present

Layer one is the core — photo-solving, where every student starts. This is what it looked like: one long block of text. Easy to copy, hard to learn anything from, and a toolbar of features sitting inside the chat. [space]
We built two versions. Plan A keeps the answer on top with the working underneath. Plan B leads with the approach and puts the answer right below it, with each step opening on tap. [space]
We wanted Plan B, because we wanted students to actually learn something. But speed was our moat — a student at eleven at night wants the answer now, and putting anything above it is a risk. So we A/B tested, and there was no drop: the approach block is short, so the answer is still one glance away. That's the trade we made — a second of attention, for a reason to read. And on the right you can see what else changed: one clean chat, the tools moved into a sidebar, and every answer now opening a next step.
The product

Scan any problem,
learn from the answer.

Answer.AI is an AI learning app: students photograph a homework problem and get an instant, step-by-step solution.

7 million students. I led the app's full redesign, from direction to ship.

→
To start with the product itself: the core loop is really simple. You scan a problem, and you get a step-by-step answer. That's what students came to us for, and for a while, that was genuinely enough.

Our users are seven million Gen Z students — about five million in high school and two million in college. Most of them are in mid-American towns, where instant help was either expensive or far away. And what they're facing is always the same: tonight's homework, Friday's test, and the GPA that decides where they get in. So their typical flow is simple: I'm stuck on this one, I snap a photo, I get the answer — and if I still don't get it, I ask a follow-up.
The honeymoon · 2023 — 2024

We landed in a golden window.

Free chatbots couldn't take a photo yet — GPT-4o only opened image and file upload to free users in May 2024, with caps. Snap a problem, get the steps: we owned that moment, and so did Question AI and Gauth — all of us in the App Store top 15.

Curve illustrative · milestones are real
So here's how it started. You might be wondering — how does a photo-solving app get that many users? Well, it's because in 2023 we landed in a golden window. Back then, the free chatbots couldn't read a photo yet — GPT-4o only opened image upload to free users in May 2024. So if you could snap a problem and get the steps, you owned that moment. We did, and so did Question AI and Gauth, and all of us were sitting in the App Store top fifteen. By September we'd hit six million users.
The turn · late 2024

Growth began to slow.

The capability that opened the market became table stakes — and our one-and-done loop gave students little reason to return. Retention slipped with it.

And then, look at the tail — the curve bent. What happened is that free ChatGPT could read photos now, competitors like Gauth started giving away free credits, and on our side, our loop ended at the answer. So we had acquisition, but no habit, and retention slipped with it.
The diagnosis

Fewer students chose us.
Most left after one answer.

Scan & Solve was no longer unique. Students got what they needed quickly, but rarely stayed long enough to build a habit with Answer.AI.

So that was our challenge, in one line: scan-and-solve alone wasn't enough anymore. We had to change something — and to figure out what, we went back to the students.

All three needs were real. None was an obvious answer.

So the first thing we did was go back to students. We did thirteen interviews with our users. [space] And out of those, we could see three directions. The first was SAT and ACT prep — the big tests. The second was college admissions — a lot of our students are first-generation college applicants, the school counselor is basically never available, and their parents can't really help. And the third was daily learning — the everyday need every student still has. [hover a card] And each of these had real commercial proof behind it — Acely, Crimson, Quizlet. So all three were real opportunities, and now it was on me and the product team to decide the direction and the priority.

I looked at it through two lenses. The first is strategic fit. I set up four questions and ran all three directions through them: does it fix our growth problem, does it reach the users we already have, does it build on our right to win, and does it keep all students in the picture. That last one is something I brought up myself — exam prep and admissions would read as "not for me" to two million college users, and I didn't want to lose them. And when you lay it all out, daily learning is the only one that clears every bar. [space → tab 2]
But I didn't want to decide on a matrix alone, so the second lens is behavior. I proposed two lightweight probes, to twenty percent of users, over two weeks. [hover] The AP prep demo got some real interest, but only four percent of students finished it. The Counselor and Scholar tutors got decent taps, but almost nobody came back. And for daily learning, I could already see it in the data: ninety-eight percent of students were using Scan to Learn, and fifty-six percent were asking a follow-up. So the way I read it: the behavior was already there. What was missing was the loop.
The decision

Daily learning became our first priority.

It was the most frequent need, closest to existing user behavior, and the strongest foundation for future growth.

The goal was to move from solving one question to supporting how students learn every day.

That's how daily learning became our first priority. It was the most frequent need, it was closest to what students were already doing, and it was the strongest foundation for everything else we might build later. [space] So we wrote the goal down in one sentence: move from solving one question to supporting how students learn every day. And everything I'm about to show you serves that sentence.

To make daily learning actually work, I proposed this strategy to the team — basically going from a shared answer machine to a school that knows each student. There are four layers here, and each one stands on the one below it. [hover the rows] The core is about making the answer worth learning from. Stay is about giving students reasons to come back. Layer one is the core, and this is the front door. This is the old Ask AI, where students landed after scanning a problem — one long block of text, easy to copy, but hard to learn from. [space]
So I made some changes. [hover the dots] First, I made the explanations something students can actually learn from: they see the steps first, and the copy-paste-able worked solution is tucked behind an expand arrow. That's how a teacher would teach it — by the time they get to the answer, they've already picked up how to solve it, almost without noticing. And second, every answer now opens a next step — similar questions, a video explanation, the concept behind it — so one problem turns into real understanding and practice of that concept. Because if the knowledge behind the answer isn't clear, nothing else on top of it matters.
Layer 02 · Stay

Give students a reason to come back tomorrow.

We kept only the methods with proven demand — and the one that surprised us had nothing to do with studying better. It was company.

Why study rooms · A trend, not a guess

“Learn with me” was already how students studied.

Our EdTech marketing agent flagged it: study-with-me streams were trending on TikTok and Instagram — students working alongside strangers for company and accountability. We built that feeling into the app.

41.7B#studywithme views on TikTok, Sep 2026
40,000people in Lofi Girl's study stream at any moment, around the clock
750K+downloads of Focustown, a study-with-friends timer, in 7 months
~$30K/moest. Focustown revenue — Pro at $9.99/mo, 4.8★ from 6K ratings

Studying alongside peers lowers test anxiety and raises self-efficacy — Frontiers in Psychology, 2025.

Layer two is about staying. Three things: study rooms for company while you work, flashcards for daily memory, and mock tests for exams. [hover a card] Each of them is performing well, but the first one is the one I want to spend time on. Flashcards, for example — students who build a deck in their first week retain eleven percent better. Mock tests: sessions go up thirty-eight percent in exam weeks, and those users convert to Pro at twice the rate. And study rooms — students who join one spend twenty-eight percent longer in the app, and our most focused student sat in a room for five and a half hours straight. [click Study rooms]
You might be wondering — why would we build this feature? It started as a signal from our marketing AI agent: "study with me" was already a huge trend. It's over forty billion views on TikTok, and Lofi Girl's study stream has something like forty thousand people in it at any given moment. And it's commercial too — there's a new study-with-friends app called Focustown, and it's already making around thirty K a month. So we built that feeling into the app, and students have really taken to it — average time in the app went up noticeably for students who join rooms, and our most active student sat in a room and focused for five and a half hours straight.

So layer two really came down to one question: not which study features to build, but why anyone would ever start using them. We had built a Study Hub before — a place to go and study. The clicks never came, because opening it asks for motivation a student doesn't have when they're stuck on one problem at eleven at night. [tap the Ask AI screen] So instead of a destination, the entries moved to where the traffic already is — right after the answer. Once a problem is explained, we read what they need: if the concept is worth keeping, it becomes flashcards; if they've just covered this material or mention an exam, we offer a mock exercise; if they say they're staying to work, we open a study room. Same features. Different moment.

A light reward system keeps it going: scanning problems, mastering topics and finishing Ace’s exercises earn badges — plus random outfit drops for an avatar. The avatar matters because of where it shows up: the study room, where other students can see it. Rewards you wear in front of someone work differently from rewards you collect alone.

And layer four is personal. To make anything personal, we first had to actually know the student — so step one is collecting their data. Before, we asked students to fill in a Me page themselves, and almost nobody did. Now the tutors pick it up naturally from conversation: what they're studying, what they're struggling with, what they're aiming for. [hover step 1] And once we know them, step two is acting on it — a check-in after a hard question, a preview the night before class, or a call with Alma when it's time to plan for college. [hover step 2] And personal messages get tapped about four times more than generic reminders. [space → tab 2]
And on top of that, we added a small incentive system — badges and an avatar — just to help students feel like this is their own personal school, not a tool they use.
A takeaway

Interviews are polite. Behaviour is honest.

We tested AP prep and a set of specialist agents. Every interview was glowing — useful, they'd use it weekly. Behaviour said otherwise, and those bets moved down the roadmap.

Interviews answer why. Data answers whether.

Ask in an interview
  • Context — what their day actually looks like
  • Workarounds — what they built to get around us
  • Language — the words they use for it
  • Emotion — what they're proud of, what embarrasses them
  • The why behind a number — why the funnel drops
Read in the data
  • Demand — a fake door beats ten interviews
  • Scale — how many, how often
  • Winner — which version actually wins
  • Willingness — what they pay for
  • Impact — whether the change moved anything

Ask what they did last week, not what they'd do next month.

One takeaway from this project. We tested AP prep and a set of specialist agents, and every interview was glowing — useful, they'd use it weekly. Behaviour said otherwise, and those bets moved down the roadmap. [space]
What I took from it isn't "don't do research" — it's that the two answer different questions. Interviews answer why. Data answers whether. So I use interviews for context, for the workarounds people invent, for the words they use, for where the emotion is. And when the question is "should we build this at all", I'd rather spend a day on a fake door than a week on interviews. Ask what they did last week, not what they'd do next month.
